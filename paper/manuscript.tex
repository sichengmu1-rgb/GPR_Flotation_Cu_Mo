\title{The operating window for copper--molybdenum separation: a Gaussian
process model of concentrate grade, and of what it does not control}

\author{Sicheng Mu\corref{cor1}}
\ead{sichengmu2026@163.com}
\affiliation{organization={School of Resources Engineering},
  addressline={Xi'an University of Architecture and Technology},
  city={Xi'an}, postcode={710055}, state={Shaanxi}, country={China}}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}

\begin{abstract}
A variogram-based Gaussian process model is fitted to twenty-nine bench
flotation tests from a copper--molybdenum concentrator and inverted for
the operating conditions that minimise the copper penalty in the
molybdenum concentrate. Two points of method are settled first. The
campaign is not one-factor-at-a-time but three two-factor planes sharing
a grind-fineness axis, so a kriging surface is recoverable on each; and
the response is concentrate grade rather than recovery, because the
archived grades satisfy the mass balance throughout while the archived
copper recoveries do not. Both grades are predictable from grind
fineness, pulp pH, diesel dosage and conditioning time, and the surfaces
recover an interaction between fineness and diesel dosage that a
single-length-scale model cannot represent. Conditioning time is inert.
The inversion gives an operating window at the upper bounds of the
tested ranges, corroborated by the nearest measured test and by
resampling of the data. A limitation of the study is that none of the
four operating variables correlates strongly with the separation,
whereas pulp rheology and concentrate wettability vary strongly with
clay content and temperature, the correlation between wettability and
temperature changing from negative to positive when clay is present.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Copper in a molybdenum concentrate is a smelter penalty that cannot be
removed downstream. Molybdenite and chalcopyrite are both naturally
hydrophobic, so reagents that float one also float the other, and the
separation rests on small differences in floatability. A plant can
adjust few variables: grind fineness, pulp pH, collector dosage and
conditioning time. A campaign that varies these and assays the
concentrate supports a response surface from which untested settings can
be proposed.

Gaussian process regression is suited to this, being defined on small
datasets and returning a posterior variance as part of its output.
Eskanlou, Yin and Caers derive the prior covariance function from
empirical variograms in the space of the operating variables and
demonstrate the approach on a designed flotation campaign, mapping a
Pareto-efficient frontier back to process settings. This study applies
that framework to a plant campaign of a different character, and reports
both what the resulting surface supports and what it does not.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

Twenty-nine rougher flotation tests were run at a molybdenum
concentrator at Jinduicheng, Shaanxi Province, on a $300$~g feed
assaying $0.5436$~\% molybdenum and $0.0364$~\% copper. Grind fineness
ranged from $30$ to $65$~\% passing $0.074$~mm, pulp pH from $3.5$ to
$11$, diesel dosage from $50$ to $200$~g/t, and diesel--pulp
conditioning time from $5$ to $25$~min. Molybdenum grade in the rougher
concentrate ranged from $1.10$ to $6.67$~\% and copper grade from
$0.215$ to $0.834$~\%.

Each reagent was swept from the same two anchor values of grind
fineness, $40$~\% and $65$~\%, and the fineness sweep was run at the
anchor values of the reagents. The design is therefore three two-factor
planes sharing a fineness axis --- fineness against pH, against diesel
dosage and against conditioning time, with fifteen, fifteen and
seventeen tests respectively --- and fineness--reagent interactions are
identified (Fig.~\ref{fig:data}). Reagent--reagent interactions are not
sampled.

The response is the concentrate grade rather than the recovery. With a
$300$~g feed at $0.5436$~\% molybdenum, concentrate mass multiplied by
molybdenum grade reproduces the reported molybdenum recovery in all
twenty-nine tests; the copper recovery is reproduced in fifteen, and
therefore depends on a feed assay not archived with the test. Grade is a
direct measurement of the concentrate. A second check against the
figures published from the same archive leaves copper grade unchanged in
all twenty-nine tests and differs in seven molybdenum values, in each of
which the archived value satisfies the mass balance.

Three production lines were surveyed over $50$ to $75$~\% fineness,
giving eighteen grade measurements that are not used to fit the model;
because all three were surveyed at the same six levels, they also give
the plant pure error. Pulp viscosity was measured against temperature,
shear rate, and clay identity and loading, for chlorite, illite,
muscovite and kaolinite, each condition twice; contact angle was
measured in a clean system and in one containing $25$~\% chlorite,
against temperature, chlorite content and diesel dosage.

\section{Model}

Each response is modelled as a Gaussian process with an automatic
relevance determination covariance function,
\begin{equation}
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\left(
-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{(x_i-x_i')^2}{\ell_i^2}\right)
+ \sigma_n^2\delta_{xx'},
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
with predictors scaled to the unit interval and all hyperparameters,
including the nugget $\sigma_n^2$, obtained by maximising the marginal
likelihood. A non-zero nugget is required: flotation is not a
deterministic simulator.

Fits are judged by leave-one-out cross-validation, reported as a
coefficient of determination $Q^2$ and a root-mean-square error, and by
the loss in $Q^2$ when one predictor is withheld. Two-dimensional
surfaces are slices through the fitted four-dimensional model at the
anchor values of the two factors not shown; a separate two-parameter fit
on each plane cross-validates worse, with $Q^2$ between $-0.01$ and
$0.40$. An operating window is sought over $160{,}000$ grid points.
Molybdenum grade is to be raised and copper grade lowered, so the
non-dominated set is the set of settings for which no other setting
improves one grade without worsening the other, and a recommendation is
taken from it as the point closest to the ideal. Its stability is
examined by resampling the data rather than by drawing Gaussian
realisations, which are not constrained to be positive.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Prediction of grade}

Both grades are predictable, copper more so than molybdenum
(Table~\ref{tab:loo}, Fig.~\ref{fig:val}). The fitted nugget accounts
for $0.34$ of the total variance of molybdenum grade and $0.43$ of
copper grade; the empirical variograms of Fig.~\ref{fig:vario}, which do
not absorb short-scale structure that the kernel cannot fit, put the
unstructured fraction at $0.21$ for both. Residuals are $1.16$ and
$0.10$ percentage points against observed standard deviations of $1.56$
and $0.147$.

The two criteria rank the predictors identically for molybdenum grade,
where pH and fineness are comparable and diesel is third. For copper
grade they disagree: the inverse length scales of Fig.~\ref{fig:val}c
rank fineness last, at $0.127$ against $0.675$ for diesel, whereas
withholding fineness costs more $Q^2$ than withholding anything except
pH. The fineness sweep covers $30$ to $65$~\% in five steps and contains
a sharp maximum, which one length scale cannot express without
shortening the others, so the length scale is not a reliable sensitivity
measure for this variable. Conditioning time contributes nothing under
either criterion --- its length scale sits at the bound of the
optimisation range and withholding it changes $Q^2$ by $0.000$ for both
responses --- although grades along the time series vary by more than a
factor of two. That variation is scatter.

\subsection{The three planes}

The fineness $\times$ pH plane has a single optimum at high pH and low
fineness, at which both grades peak. The fineness $\times$ diesel plane
contains an interaction: at the low fineness anchor, molybdenum grade
falls monotonically with diesel addition, from $6.67$~\% at $50$~g/t to
$2.05$~\% at $200$~g/t, whereas at the high fineness anchor it rises
from $2.47$ to $4.02$~\% over the same range (Fig.~\ref{fig:surf}).
Neither structure is representable by a single length scale per
variable.

\subsection{The operating window}

The two grades correlate at $+0.678$, so the trade-off of
Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}a is the boundary of a cloud; the non-dominated set
contains $747$ of the $160{,}000$ grid points. The recommended
conditions are given in Table~\ref{tab:window}, with the mean, standard
deviation and probability of meeting a copper target in
Fig.~\ref{fig:ens}. Both the fineness and the diesel dosage lie at the
upper bounds of the tested ranges, which is why the plant surveys are
needed.

Three checks apply. The nearest measured test, test~20, was run at the
same fineness, pH and diesel dosage with a conditioning time of
$15$~min and returned $4.02$~\% molybdenum and $0.328$~\% copper, so the
predicted grades agree with a measurement at adjacent settings to within
$0.02$ percentage points of copper. Refitting from eight random
initialisations returns the same point. Resampling places the copper
grade achievable subject to a molybdenum grade of at least $3.5$~\% at
$0.329 \pm 0.041$~\%. At a molybdenum target of $4$~\% the best
achievable copper grade rises to $0.416$~\%, so the constraint binds.

\subsection{Transfer to the plant}

The bench design ends at $65$~\% fineness, so the surveys locate the
optimum relative to that boundary. All three lines reach their maximum
molybdenum grade at $65$~\% and their minimum copper grade between $70$
and $75$~\%, and molybdenum grade falls sharply at $75$~\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}); with the bench model this places the optimum
at $65$ to $70$~\%. The three lines give a pooled standard deviation of
$0.82$ percentage points of molybdenum grade and $0.053$ of copper grade
on fifteen degrees of freedom, against a spread of $4.08$ and $0.215$
across the fineness range --- a signal-to-noise ratio of about five for
molybdenum and four for copper.

\subsection{The pulp environment}

Apparent viscosity falls with temperature for chlorite by $15.8$~\% over
$5$ to $35$~$^\circ$C, for muscovite by $12.3$~\% and for illite by
$7.0$~\%. Kaolinite gives about $385$~mPa~s where the others give
$180$--$205$, and falls by only $2.5$~\%; its replicates differ by
$32$~mPa~s against $5$ to $9$ for the others, so the correct statement
is the absence of a detectable temperature response rather than a
smaller one. A clean concentrate loses hydrophobicity on heating, its
contact angle falling from $82.8^\circ$ at $10$~$^\circ$C to $67.8^\circ$
at $50$~$^\circ$C; with $25$~\% chlorite it gains hydrophobicity over
the same interval, from $85.6^\circ$ to $91.3^\circ$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pulp}). The sign of the temperature coefficient therefore
reverses with clay present, and the reversal is established by the time
a quarter of the solids are clay. Diesel dosage acts in the same
direction and to a similar optimum near $100$~g/t in both systems, but
the effect is twice as large without clay.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}

The recommended window is supported by a measured test at adjacent
settings, by refitting, and by the fineness optimum of three plant
surveys, and should be read against the uncertainty of
Fig.~\ref{fig:ens}. The quantity the decision depends on, the copper
grade achievable subject to a molybdenum constraint, is better
determined than the settings that deliver it.

The operating variables do not control the separation. Molybdenum and
copper grade correlate at $+0.678$, and their ratio is not predictable
from any of the four: fitted directly to the ratio the same covariance
function returns $Q^2 = -0.072$, and forming the ratio from the two
leave-one-out predictions returns $Q^2 = -0.002$. Two independent routes
agreeing on a negative result exclude an artefact of a particular fit,
though not an effect smaller than the dataset can resolve. The
interaction in the fineness $\times$ diesel plane is not an exception,
since it displaces both grades together.

The pulp measurements indicate where selectivity is instead determined.
The concentrator faces a colder pulp and a different clay loading in
winter, and the two act in opposing directions: heating lowers the
viscosity penalty but also lowers the contact angle of a clean
concentrate and raises that of one carrying chlorite. Kaolinite, both
the most viscous clay and the one whose viscosity does not respond to
temperature, cannot be assisted by heating. Converting this mechanism
into a control strategy requires flotation tests at controlled
temperature with the clay mineralogy characterised.

The data come from a single deposit, so the numerical results are local,
and reagent--reagent interactions are unidentified.
